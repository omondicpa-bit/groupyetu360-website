Merry-Go-Round (Table Banking) Explained: Rules, Rotation, and Common Disputes

Table banking looks simple until it isn't. Here's how rotation actually works, the rules every group should agree on before the first contribution, and why disputes happen even among people who trust each other.

GroupYetu360 Team · Updated 2026

Table banking and merry-go-rounds (the terms are mostly used interchangeably in Kenya) are probably the oldest form of organised group savings in the country. Older than mobile money, older than most SACCOs. The mechanics are simple enough to explain in one sentence: members contribute a fixed amount on a set schedule, and the pooled amount goes to a different member each round until everyone has received a payout once.

Simple in theory. In practice, it's one of the most dispute-prone group structures there is. Not because people are dishonest, but because the rules that prevent disputes are usually agreed verbally, once, and then everyone relies on memory for the next twelve months.

How rotation actually works

Most groups use one of three rotation methods, and it's worth being explicit about which one your group has chosen:

  • Fixed order. Decided once at the start, usually by agreement or seniority, and followed strictly round after round.
  • Drawn lots. The order is randomised at the first meeting, usually by picking numbers or names from a container, so nobody can be accused of favouritism.
  • Need-based. Members bid or request to receive a particular round based on an upcoming expense (school fees, a medical bill), with the group agreeing case by case.

None of these is "correct". But a group that never explicitly picked one, and instead just fell into a pattern, is the group most likely to have a dispute the first time someone wants to change the order.

The rules worth writing down before round one

Write these down, even informally in a notebook or a shared document. A verbal agreement made in an excited first meeting is rarely remembered the same way by everyone six months later.
  • Contribution amount and due date. Fixed forever, or does it increase with membership growth? Say so explicitly.
  • What happens when someone misses a contribution. Does their payout get delayed? Is there a penalty? Can another member cover it temporarily?
  • Whether the pool can be used for internal loans between rounds, and if so, what interest rate applies and how it's tracked.
  • What happens if a member wants to leave mid-cycle. Before or after they've received their payout.
  • Who verifies that a contribution actually happened before the group treats it as paid.

The three disputes that come up again and again

"It wasn't my turn yet"

This happens most often in need-based rotation, where the order isn't fixed and memory of "who agreed to what" diverges between members. It also happens in fixed-order groups when someone joins mid-cycle and the group never clearly recorded where they slot in.

"I already paid this round"

The most common version of this: a member sends an M-Pesa payment directly to the treasurer's personal number. The treasurer receives dozens of similar messages that week. By the time the notebook gets updated, the record doesn't match what the member remembers sending.

"The interest on my loan isn't what I agreed to"

Groups that lend from the pooled fund between rounds often calculate interest by hand, inconsistently, especially when a loan is partially repaid over several rounds. Small calculation drift compounds into a dispute that feels much bigger than the shillings involved.

Why digital tracking specifically helps here

The common thread across all three disputes above isn't dishonesty. It's that the record of what happened lived in someone's memory, a scattered set of M-Pesa messages, or a notebook that wasn't updated in real time. The fix isn't stricter rules on paper. It's removing the gap between "a contribution happened" and "everyone can see that it happened."

When rotation order, contribution status, and any internal loan balances are tracked in one place, every member can check them independently. Not just the treasurer. The three disputes above mostly stop happening, because there's nothing left to disagree about. The record is the record.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a group run more than one merry-go-round at once?

Yes, and many established groups do. For example a monthly table banking pool alongside a separate welfare fund. The important part is keeping the two pots clearly separate in whatever record-keeping method you use, since mixing them is one of the fastest ways to create a dispute nobody can untangle.

What's a fair way to decide rotation order for a new group?

Drawn lots tend to cause the fewest disputes long-term, since no one can later claim the order favoured anyone. Whatever method you choose, agree it once, in front of everyone, and write it down.

Should merry-go-round funds ever be lent out with interest?

Many groups do this successfully as a way to grow the pool, but it adds real bookkeeping complexity. If your group does this, treat loan tracking as seriously as contribution tracking. It's usually the source of the most complicated disputes.
