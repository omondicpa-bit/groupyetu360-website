How to Start and Manage a Community Group in Kenya

A practical, no-fluff guide to starting a welfare group, investment club, chama or SACCO in Kenya - the roles you need, the money model to pick, and the mistakes that quietly kill most groups within their first year.

GroupYetu360 Team · Updated 2026

Kenya runs on community groups. Estimates put the number of active groups - chamas, welfare associations, investment clubs, table banking circles, SACCOs - at close to 300,000, managing well over Ksh 300 billion between them. If you're reading this, you're probably one of the people about to start one, or trying to fix one that's already wobbling.

This guide walks through the whole process: what kind of group to form, how to structure leadership, how to handle money without it becoming a source of conflict, and when to formalise. It applies whether you're calling your group a chama, a welfare group, an investment club, a table banking circle, or a SACCO in the making - in Kenya, "chama" is really just the Swahili word for "group, " and it covers all of these. The merry-go-round is one form a chama can take; it isn't the only one, and it's often the least sophisticated.

1. Decide what kind of group you're actually building

The single biggest cause of group breakdown is skipping this step - people join with different expectations of what the money is for, and the disagreement surfaces months later when it's expensive to fix. Before you recruit a single member, agree on the model.

ModelHow it worksBest for
Merry-go-roundFixed contribution each cycle, full pot paid to one member on rotationShort-term, low-complexity groups; quick wins to build trust
Welfare groupMembers contribute to a shared fund used for emergencies - medical, bereavement, school feesGroups formed around mutual support rather than growth
Table bankingMembers save into a common pool, then borrow from it at an agreed interest rateGroups wanting to build a lending fund and grow member savings over time
Investment clubPooled capital is invested - land, shares, money market funds, small businessGroups with a long horizon and a clear investment thesis
SACCOFormally registered cooperative, regulated by the SACCO Societies Regulatory AuthorityEstablished groups that have outgrown informal structures

Many mature groups run more than one of these at once - a welfare fund alongside table banking, for instance. That's fine, as long as the money for each is tracked separately from day one. Mixing an emergency welfare pot with investment capital is how groups end up unable to answer a simple question: "how much do we actually have, and whose is it?"

2. Set your leadership structure before you set your contribution amount

Most Kenyan groups use a version of the same three officer roles. Resist the temptation to skip any of them, even in a group of six friends.

  • Chairperson - runs meetings, resolves disputes, is the external face of the group.
  • Secretary - keeps the constitution, minutes, and membership register.
  • Treasurer - the single most scrutinised role in any group. Handles contributions, payouts, and reporting. This is also the role most associated with disputes when records are kept on paper or in a personal notebook - not necessarily because of dishonesty, but because manual records are hard to verify, easy to lose, and slow to reconcile when someone asks "where did my contribution go?"

Larger or investment-focused groups often add an Assistant Treasurer and an Investment/Projects Committee. Whatever you choose, write it into a simple constitution - even one page - covering membership criteria, contribution amounts and deadlines, penalties for late payment, how officers are elected, and how disputes are resolved. Groups that skip this step almost always end up drafting it later, under worse circumstances, after a disagreement has already happened.

3. Build trust into the money flow, not just the people

Trust in a group isn't really about how well members know each other - most disputes happen between people who trusted each other completely at the start. What actually prevents conflict is a money flow every member can independently verify, so no one has to simply take the treasurer's word for it.

The three questions every member should be able to answer at any time, without asking the treasurer: How much have I personally contributed so far? What is the group's current total balance? Who is due to receive the next payout or loan?

Groups that keep contributions in a shared M-Pesa till or personal account, tracked in a notebook or a shared spreadsheet, usually manage fine for the first few months - while membership is small and everyone still checks in regularly. The friction shows up as the group grows past 15–20 members, or after the first missed contribution, or the first payout dispute. That's the point where most groups either formalise their record-keeping or start losing members to frustration.

4. Registration: when informal is fine, and when it isn't

Most chamas operate informally for years, and that's entirely legal - Kenya doesn't require a welfare group or small investment club to register with anyone. Registration becomes worth considering once a group needs to:

  • Open a group bank account (most banks require a certificate of registration as a Community Based Organisation or Self-Help Group)
  • Buy land or property in the group's name rather than an individual's
  • Apply for credit facilities or grants that require formal status
  • Grow large enough that SACCO registration and regulatory oversight makes sense

Registering as a Self-Help Group or CBO is done through the Department of Social Development and is relatively straightforward for an established group with a constitution and a membership list already in place - another reason to write the constitution early rather than reconstructing it from memory later.

5. The mistakes that quietly sink groups

No separation between roles and money

If the treasurer is also the only signatory on the bank account with no oversight, the group is one bad month - or one bad decision - away from a crisis. Dual signatories, or a system where every contribution and payout is visible to all members in real time, closes this gap without requiring anyone to distrust the treasurer personally.

No clear consequence for late or missed contributions

Ambiguity here is corrosive. Agree the penalty in advance, write it down, and apply it consistently - including to founding members.

Manual records that only one person can read

A notebook or personal spreadsheet works until it doesn't - when the treasurer is unreachable, when a member disputes a figure, or when the group needs to produce records for a bank or a new member. Digital, member-visible records remove the single point of failure.

No plan for what happens when a member leaves

Especially in investment and table banking groups: what happens to accumulated shares, outstanding loans, or welfare contributions if a member exits? Decide this before it happens to someone, not after.

6. Where technology actually helps - and where it doesn't

A group of six people meeting monthly in someone's living room doesn't need software. The point where digital tools start paying for themselves is usually around 15–20 members, or whenever contributions start coming in on different days from different channels - M-Pesa, cash, bank transfer - and reconciling them by hand starts eating real time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a lawyer to start a chama or welfare group?

No. A one-page constitution agreed and signed by founding members is enough for most informal groups. Legal help becomes worthwhile once you're registering formally or handling significant investment capital.

What's a reasonable group size to start with?

Most successful groups start between 8 and 20 members - small enough that trust and accountability are easy to maintain, large enough that contributions add up to something meaningful.

Can one group run a merry-go-round and an investment fund at the same time?

Yes, and many do. The requirement is that the two pots are tracked completely separately, with their own records, so members always know which fund they're contributing to and what it's for.

Is a chama the same as a SACCO?

No. A chama is informal by default; a SACCO is a formally registered, regulated cooperative. A chama can grow into a SACCO once it reaches the size and structure to justify formal registration.
